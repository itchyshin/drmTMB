## Findings

Individual persistent heterogeneity has direct CJS precedent. Giménez and Choquet (2010) put normally distributed individual effects on survival and/or recapture logits, integrate the effect over each observed history using Gauss-Hermite quadrature, and maximize the marginal likelihood. This supports the model class and a fixed-node quadrature comparator. It does not establish that the planned Julia/R kernels, higher-dimensional mixed shared/individual models, or uncertainty procedures are correct.

There is also a distinct multilevel capture-recapture precedent. Choquet et al. (2013) model individuals nested within independent clusters, with random effects and covariance structures spanning individual and cluster levels. They integrate cluster-specific likelihood contributions by standard Gauss-Hermite quadrature in E-SURGE. Their formulation is useful precedent for dependence within clusters, but it is not the proposed combination of persistent individual effects with shared site/year effects, and does not validate that combined integral or its Hessian. The frozen contract deliberately refuses shared-plus-individual combinations in this slice.

Herliansyah, King, and King (2022) provide the most relevant integration comparison, with an important split between closed-population Mh and open CJS. For closed Mh, their simulations compare Laplace orders and Gauss-Hermite quadrature; fourth-order Laplace performs similarly to fine-grid GHQ in the studied scenarios, whereas second-order Laplace can be less accurate. For open CJS with continuous time-varying covariates, they compare second-order multivariate Laplace with an HMM discretization and report similar estimates/coverage and faster Laplace computation in their simulated and case-study settings. They do not test fourth-order Laplace for that open CJS case. These results justify a targeted comparator and caution against extrapolation: they do not establish accuracy or speed for persistent Gaussian individual effects plus shared effects or the planned reference-selection ratio.

TMB provides automatic differentiation and Laplace machinery for marginal likelihoods defined by a joint data/random-effect likelihood. That computational infrastructure leaves the suitability of a particular CJS latent structure, normalization, integration dimension, and approximation to be established. Herliansyah et al. specifically use TMB for numerical derivatives around the recursive CJS likelihood, where higher analytic derivatives are difficult. Their application provides a computational precedent; this implementation still needs its own benchmark.

The log-SD regression in the contract has adjacent precedent in mixed-effects location-scale models. Hedeker and Nordgren (2013) describe covariates affecting between-subject and within-subject variances in Gaussian longitudinal/clustered outcomes. This establishes predictor-dependent variance as a general model class. Identifiability, interpretation, and finite-sample performance remain open for covariates predicting the SD of a CJS survival/detection random effect. Those require dedicated design checks and simulation.

Reference selection is substantively motivated by survivorship selection. Sarzo, King, and McCrea (2026) state in the accessible abstract that conditioning on first observation can induce survivorship bias when survival to initial capture selects individuals. Their article title and abstract directly concern correcting the random-effect distribution for this mechanism. The full text could not be read from the available source, so the contract's exact normalized ratio
